\subsection{The particulate ratio, sampled rather than asserted}

Residential fuel burning emits \SI{282.7}{\kilo\tonne} of primary \PM{} against road
transport's exhaust \PM{} of \SI{5.8}{\kilo\tonne} under the reference---49 times as much
(parametric 95 percent interval 14--199)---and \SI{631.9}{\kilo\tonne}, \num{109} times
(31--445), under configuration L, from a sector less than half road transport's size in
\COe{} either way. The interval's width is the honest print of the road layer's EMEP/EEA
Tier~1 factor spans, which cover uncontrolled to modern technology for an unmeasured fleet;
the superseded per-kilometre diagnostic put the ratio near thirty on factors with no located
provenance. Within the national inventory this sector belongs to, household biomass is roughly
the largest single source of primary \PM{} under either configuration---roughly
two-thirds of the inventory's total under the reference---ahead of open waste burning at
\SI{63.9}{\kilo\tonne}, crop-residue field burning at \SI{54.0}{} and transport.

The reason this comparison can be asserted rather than hedged is structural. Cross-source
particulate comparisons in developing countries usually assemble estimates from separate
studies with separate activity bases, so the ratio between two sources is partly an artefact
of which datasets each author had. Every air layer in this inventory is computed from its own
sector's greenhouse-gas activity data---the same fuel, the same tonnage, the same population
panel---which removes that mismatched-activity confounder. What survives in the ratio is
everything else: the activity configuration (Section~\ref{sec:results}), the factor
families, and the pollutant and boundary definitions, all stated rather than assumed away.
Under matched years and those stated choices, the reference configuration gives the ratio
above; it is conditional on them, not a physical constant.

\subsection{Black carbon}

At \SI{20.0}{\kilo\tonne} (\SI{41.5}{} under configuration~L), household biomass is the dominant Pakistani source of black carbon
in this inventory. It is reported as mass and deliberately not converted to \COe{}: its
metrics remain contested, its lifetime is days, and a \COe{} figure would imply a comparability
with long-lived gases that the science does not support.

That said, the conventional \COe{} total does not capture the sector's short-lived
climate-forcing emissions: it is simultaneously the country's largest source of black
carbon and of organic carbon, whose forcing effects oppose each other, and no net
forcing calculation is presented here. The limitation is that the greenhouse-gas
number describes only part of the sector's climate relevance, not that its direction
of error is known.

\subsection{The policy inversion}

The greenhouse-gas and primary-emission rankings of Pakistani sources are close to
inverted. A
mitigation programme selected on \COe{} would target agriculture, transport and electricity; a
programme selected on population exposure would target household cooking and heating first by a
wide margin, followed by two open-burning categories that are together \SI{1.0}{\percent} of
the national inventory.

This is not an argument that greenhouse-gas accounting is the wrong lens. It is an argument
that a single inventory cannot rank interventions for both objectives, and that a country
choosing air-quality interventions from a greenhouse-gas ranking will systematically
under-prioritise the sources that matter most for health.

\subsection{Limitations of this layer}

The factors are literature values, not Pakistani measurements: Andreae's field
compilation for the biomass streams, whose cookstove emissions vary by a factor of several
with stove design, fuel moisture and operator behaviour, and the EMEP/EEA small-combustion
Tier~1 factors \citep{emep2023smallcombustion} for gas, LPG, kerosene and coal---the
chapter now held---contributing \SI{16.4}{\kilo\tonne} of \NOx{} and under one percent
of the biomass \PM{}. Generators carry no air rows: no household-genset factor class
exists, a declared omission bounded by that stream's 56 kt of fuel. Two factor
sensitivities are published in the scenarios table: the technology mix (the reference
assumes the traditional/open stove class throughout, consistent with the MTF typology's
rural dominance; up to ten percent improved-class adoption would lower \PM{} to
\SI{260}{\kilo\tonne}), and the residue factor family (the crop-residue rows apply
field-burning factors as the nearest available class for stove combustion---a
provisional assignment; the domestic-biofuel family would give \SI{274}{}).

And the layer reports \emph{gross emitted mass} at the appliance, not mass reaching outdoor air
and not exposure. Some indoor particulate deposits before reaching the ambient atmosphere,
while personal exposure depends on ventilation, stove placement and behaviour; separating
gross emission, outdoor release and exposure requires release fractions and an exposure
model this paper does not attempt (the deferred exposure layer in the gap register). The
direction of the exposure difference against transport---household emissions released
inside and around dwellings, transport emissions dispersing from roadways---is plausible
but is not quantified here.
