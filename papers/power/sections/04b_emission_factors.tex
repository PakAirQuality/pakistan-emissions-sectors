\subsection{Table 2.2 for the grid, Table 2.3 for captive}

IPCC Volume~2 Chapter~2 publishes four parallel default tables for stationary combustion: energy
industries, manufacturing industries and construction, commercial, and residential. Grid
generation is 1A1a and takes Table~2.2. Captive self-generation serves the factory that owns
it rather than selling power, so it is 1A2 and takes Table~2.3.

Captive gas takes Table~2.3, the manufacturing-industries table. For natural gas its
factors are identical to Table~2.2's, so the choice carries no numerical weight here; it is
cited correctly because the citation is what an auditor checks, and because it will matter if
the captive fleet later resolves into oil- and coal-fired plants, where the tables do differ
\citep{ahmad2026national}.

\subsection{The coal blend as a parameter, not a decision}

The lignite share is \SI{79}{\percent}, read from the plant fuel ledger: the Thar-named
mine-mouth plants are \SI{75.2}{\percent} of FY2023--24 coal generation and the
imported-coal plants \SI{16.4}{\percent}, with one residual---Lucky Electric, at
\SI{8.4}{\percent}, whose name states no origin. The residual is carried as bounds rather
than assigned: \num{0.752} with Lucky as imported, \num{0.835} with Lucky as Thar, and
\num{0.79} is their midpoint. An independent route---plant coal mass weighted by IPCC rank
calorific values---gives \numrange{0.70}{0.84}, overlapping the generation route, which is
what makes the range defensible without resolving the plant. The heat-rate channel of
Section~\ref{sec:impliedcv} has since produced evidence on the plant itself: Lucky's
implied calorific value sits at \SIrange{11.4}{12.1}{} TJ/Gg in all three of its
years---lignite, not imported bituminous---which favours the upper bound. The bounds are
kept until a source states the origin outright, but the residual is no longer
evidence-free.

This replaces an inference from an older fleet. The previous value of \SI{35}{\percent} was
read off an earlier year's generation mix, before Thar Energy, ThalNova and Lucky entered
service; by the anchor year the mine-mouth plants dominate. The parameter now carries
\SI{12}{\percent} uncertainty, spanning the union of both routes.

Because the calculation is on an energy basis, that uncertainty propagates to about
\SI{1}{\percent} on the coal factor rather than to a factor of two. This is the practical
payoff of Section~\ref{sec:methods}, and the blend remains a parameter with a published
range rather than a decision the compiler must defend.

\subsection{The TOE conversion, which is this sector's largest unresolved risk}

The tonnes-of-oil-equivalent constant is \SI{0.041868}{} TJ per tonne, cited to the Eurostat
definition---a \emph{net} calorific basis, matching the IPCC factors. This is not an IPCC
value; the 2006 Guidelines work in TJ/Gg and never define TOE, so it must be cited elsewhere.

The risk is that NEPRA's own TOE convention is unverified. If the regulator converts on a
\emph{gross} basis, as some South Asian statistical practice does, this sector overstates
\COtwo{} by roughly \SI{5}{\percent} for coal and oil and \SI{10}{\percent} for gas. A
one-sided conversion error cannot be expressed as a symmetric sensitivity without implying
a symmetry that does not exist---so it is not folded into any $\pm$ percentage. It is
instead a named structural case: the gross-basis scenario computes to
\SI{37.17}{\mega\tonne} against the \SI{40.04}{} family reference (alternatives 38.45 heat-rate, 44.05 with K-Electric, 57.82 legacy control)
(Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}), with its own conditional interval and no probability
attached. The evidence leans net---the MMBTU-metered plants imply \SI{48.7}{\percent}
efficiency, which only a net basis supports, and Pakistan's CRT filing states NCV
outright---but evidence is not documentation.

Resolving it requires one page: the conversion-factors table at the front of the HDIP Pakistan
Energy Yearbook. It is recorded as this sector's highest-priority acquisition.
