% ===========================================================================
\section{Architecture: The Sector Contract}
\label{sec:architecture}
% ===========================================================================

The inventory is a Python package in which each of the ten sectors is a module registered in
a single list. Registration is not a formality: it is the point at which a sector becomes
subject to \num{32} invariants that are checked against every registered sector on every
commit. A sector that cannot satisfy them does not enter the inventory.

\subsection{What a sector must provide}

The contract is deliberately small. A sector module declares a name, a reference year and the
set of IPCC categories it emits, and exposes two functions: one that loads its activity data
and emission factors, and one that turns that data into a list of component rows. A component
row is the atomic unit of the whole inventory---one gas, from one sub-item, within one
category---carrying the emission in gigagrammes, its \COe{} value on both AR5 and AR6
metrics, a relative uncertainty, and its evidence, temporal and spatial classes. The
uncertainty is resolved to \emph{parameters} rather than carried on the row: a separate
registry (\texttt{data/parameter\_scope.csv}) names the physical quantities behind each row
and which other rows share them, so one component may depend on an activity, a conversion and
a factor with three different dependency structures. That resolution is used by the
Approach-2 simulation; the Approach-1 quadrature remains row-level, which is one reason the
two are worth running against each other. Everything downstream in
the system---the national rollup, the uncertainty analysis, the key-category screen, the
completeness report, the provincial allocation, the figures---consumes component rows and
nothing else. No downstream module knows what a buffalo or a locomotive is.

The cross-sector GWP contract is explicit. On the AR5 basis used for headline totals,
CO$_2=1$, biogenic CH$_4=28$, fossil CH$_4=30$ and N$_2$O$=265$. Each component row carries
its gas origin, so agriculture and waste methane use the biogenic value while fossil-fuel
sectors use the fossil value; the national aggregator never infers origin from the sector name.
The corresponding AR6 values are stored alongside AR5 rather than substituted silently.

The consequence is that adding a sector requires no change to any analysis. Fugitive
emissions and cement were added late in the project's life; neither required a line of change
in the uncertainty, key-category or benchmark code, and both appeared in the national outputs
on first registration.

\subsection{The invariants}

Table~\ref{tab:contract} groups the \num{32} contract tests by what they protect. They are
not unit tests of any sector's arithmetic---each sector has those separately---but properties
that must hold of every sector, present and future.

Several deserve comment because they encode a mistake that was actually made or narrowly
avoided. \emph{Category names are globally unique} exists because two sectors independently
wanted the category name ``Other''; had both been allowed, the national rollup would have
summed them into one row and the key-category screen would have ranked a chimera.
\emph{Correlation groups stay within one category} exists because a correlation group that
spans categories makes the Approach-1 quadrature in the key-category analysis ill-defined:
the analysis apportions variance to categories, and a group straddling two of them has no
category to be apportioned to. \emph{\COe{} is exactly the GWP conversion} pins the
relationship between the gigagramme figure and the two \COe{} figures to an exact
multiplication, so that a sector cannot report a \COe{} value that its own gas mass does not
support---the single most common transcription error in spreadsheet inventories.
\emph{Intermediates are not cached} asserts that two consecutive builds of a sector return
equal but distinct objects, which is what makes the sensitivity analyses in several sectors
safe: they rebuild a sector under a changed assumption, and a cached intermediate would leak
the changed assumption into the baseline.

\subsection{Why a registry rather than a spreadsheet}

The proximate argument is the one above: a registry gives cross-cutting checks somewhere to
live. The deeper argument concerns what happens when the inventory is revised.

A spreadsheet inventory is revised by editing cells. The provenance of a cell---which source,
which year, which conversion---lives in the compiler's memory or in a note beside it, and the
previous value is gone. In this system every activity figure is a row in a version-controlled
CSV with a source column, every emission factor cites its IPCC table, and every revision is a
commit with a message. When the residential sector's dung-cake stream changed because
agriculture's manure-management shares changed, the resulting \SI{0.4}{\mega\tonne} movement
in the national total is attributable to a specific commit rather than being a difference
between two spreadsheet files.

That is also what makes the \emph{sensitivity} results in this paper cheap to produce. A
sensitivity analysis in a spreadsheet means copying the workbook. Here it means calling the
sector's build function with one parameter changed, which is why the residential sector can
report its total with firewood excluded, at $-\SI{35}{\percent}$ and at $+\SI{35}{\percent}$
on every run, and why the waste sector can publish both readings of the disputed sewer
methane correction factor rather than choosing one (Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}).

\subsection{Verification and reproducibility}

The engine ships \num{3882} defined test functions. Beyond the \num{32} contract invariants
and the per-sector
arithmetic tests, three categories are worth naming because they guard the manuscript rather
than the code. \emph{Provenance tests} assert that every emission factor used anywhere in the
inventory cites a document in the bibliography, and that any source declared informal is
explicitly listed as such. \emph{Number tests} parse this paper's \LaTeX{} source and check
each quoted figure against the generated CSV it came from, so a number cannot drift between
the engine and the text. \emph{Figure-currency tests} assert that every image in a manuscript
directory is byte-identical to the file the engine most recently produced.

Continuous integration runs the full suite, regenerates every output a build phase writes,
rebuilds every figure and compiles all manuscripts with \texttt{tectonic} on every push. A
manuscript that does not compile, or that quotes a number the engine does not produce, is a
build failure.

That qualification is load-bearing and this release states it rather than leaving it implied.
\num{96} of the shipped products are \emph{held}: no build phase writes them, because their
producers need network access, a GIS extra, an archive too large to commit, or simply hours.
Each is pinned by the digest it carried at release, so a reader can prove the file they have
is the file that was published --- but that digest was taken from the file itself, so it
cannot detect a producer whose output has since moved. Only \num{12} of the \num{96} are
re-derived and compared against the file on disk by a mode that writes nothing; for
\num{3} the only recorded instruction is the producer itself, which would rewrite the
product rather than check it, and the remaining \num{81} record no verification at all.
Those counts are a declared property of each row, not an inference from its command, and the
twelve are re-derived on every test run --- each announcing which products it compared, so a
product added to a producer cannot inherit a verification that does not read it. The first
run found a held provenance record whose generator disagreed with it, and the file, not the
generator, was the correct one: the stale-file warning had been inviting a regeneration that
would have replaced a true provenance claim with a false one.
